Polybutylene (Poly B) Piping in Prince George Homes: What You Need to Know

By Mike Webber · Ironclad Inspections · Prince George, BC · August 2026

If you're house-hunting in one of Prince George's older neighbourhoods, there's a good chance you'll hear the term "Poly B" at some point — either from your inspector, your realtor, or your insurance broker. It can sound alarming if you've never come across it before, but it's a well-understood issue with clear options, not a reason to panic.

This guide explains what Poly B piping is, how to recognize it, and what it actually means for you as a buyer.

"Finding Poly B in a home doesn't mean walk away. It means you go in with your eyes open and a plan."

What Is Polybutylene (Poly B) Piping?

Polybutylene, usually just called Poly B, is a flexible grey (sometimes bluish or black) plastic pipe that was widely used for residential water lines from the late 1970s through the mid-1990s. It was popular because it was inexpensive and quick to install compared to copper.

Over time, though, it became clear that Poly B doesn't hold up as well as builders expected. It's no longer manufactured or used in new construction, but it's still present behind the walls of thousands of homes across BC — including plenty here in Prince George.

How to Tell If Your Home Has Poly B

You don't need any special tools to check — just a bit of patience and a flashlight. Look in a few common spots:

Where to Look for Poly B

  • Under sinks in the kitchen and bathrooms
  • Around the hot water tank and main shutoff valve
  • In the crawlspace or basement mechanical area
  • Where pipes enter the home from outside
  • Look for grey (or occasionally black/blue) plastic pipe, often with a stamp reading "PB2110" printed along its length

If you're not sure what you're looking at, this is exactly the kind of thing a home inspection is meant to catch — it gets identified and documented as part of a standard inspection, no extra guesswork required on your part.

Why Poly B Became a Concern

The pipe itself isn't the whole story — a lot of the trouble traces back to the fittings used to join sections together. Early installations often used plastic fittings that weren't built to handle decades of water pressure and temperature swings. Over the years, chlorine and other treatment chemicals in municipal water can also gradually weaken the pipe from the inside.

The end result is that Poly B systems can develop small pinhole leaks, or in some cases fail more suddenly at a fitting. It doesn't happen in every home with Poly B, and it doesn't happen on any predictable schedule — which is exactly why it's treated as a known risk rather than an emergency.

Does Finding Poly B Mean Something Is Wrong?

Not necessarily. Plenty of homes with Poly B piping have had zero issues for years. Finding it during an inspection simply means it gets flagged as a known material with a known risk profile — the same way an inspector notes the age of a furnace or roof. It's information for you to factor into your decision, not an automatic red flag on the home itself.

Poly B and Home Insurance in BC

This is often the part that matters most to buyers. A growing number of BC insurers now ask directly about Poly B piping when quoting a policy. Depending on the insurer, having Poly B can mean a higher premium, restricted water-damage coverage, or in some cases a requirement to replace the piping before a policy is issued or renewed.

This is worth sorting out early — ideally before your subject removal date, not after. Your inspection report gives you and your insurance broker something concrete to work from, rather than a guess.

What Happens If Poly B Is Found During Your Inspection

If Poly B piping turns up during your inspection, it gets noted in your report along with photos showing where it was found. From there, you have a few practical paths forward:

Talk to your insurance broker early. Before subjects are removed is the best time to find out how a specific insurer will treat the home.

Get a plumber's quote. A licensed plumber can tell you whether a partial reroute or a full repipe makes sense, and give you a real number to work with.

Use it in your negotiation. Depending on the market and the rest of the report, this can be a reasonable point to discuss with the seller through your realtor.

Decide it's not a dealbreaker. Many buyers simply factor the eventual cost into their long-term plans for the home and move forward with confidence.

Replacement Costs — What to Expect

Costs vary quite a bit depending on the size of the home, how the pipes are routed, and whether walls need to be opened up. A partial reroute using modern PEX piping is generally on the lower end of the scale, while a full whole-home repipe with wall repair costs more. Getting two or three quotes from licensed local plumbers is the best way to get a real number for your specific home rather than relying on a general estimate.

A Note for Prince George Buyers Specifically

Poly B shows up regularly in Prince George homes built from the late 1970s through the mid-1990s, including many properties in established neighbourhoods like Spruceland, Hart Highlands, and the Bowl. It's common enough that it shouldn't be a surprise — but it's also easy to miss if you're not specifically looking for it, especially in a crawlspace or behind a finished basement wall.

Our winters and the resulting freeze-thaw cycles don't directly cause Poly B to fail, but any existing weak spots in old fittings are more likely to show themselves when pipes go through repeated temperature swings — one more reason it's worth having a professional set of eyes on it before you buy.

The Bottom Line

Poly B piping is one of the more common "older home" findings in Prince George, and it's manageable in almost every case. The goal of your inspection isn't to scare you off a home — it's to make sure you know exactly what you're working with, so you can make your decision, talk to your insurer, and plan ahead with real information instead of guesswork.
